# L7/peering/

**Object:** External relationship surface of the bounded 7LM object.
**Path:** `L7/peering/` · **Position:** L7 — SURFACE / FORWARD FACE · **Owner:** L7/
**Local question:** What identifiable external counterpart is this bounded object connected to, through what exposure and direction, and what object or interaction participates in that connection?

## What this directory is

Peering is the L7 representation of an identifiable relationship between the bounded 7LM object and something outside that boundary.

The external counterpart is the **peer**. Peering describes the relationship at the surface. It does not determine what the peer is, why the relationship exists, whether the relationship is scientific, or what scientific meaning any object participating in the relationship possesses.

A peer may be a person, group, institution, repository, archive, laboratory, publisher, standards body, computational system, model, service, instrument, organization, or another bounded object. The architecture does not require peers to be equivalent kinds of things.

The relation itself need not be scientific. It may carry scientific material, depend upon scientific work, request scientific work, communicate scientific work, support administration or review, provide a service, or have no scientific content at all.

[
	ext{peering}=	ext{represented relationship across the boundary}
]

## Why it exists

A bounded research object does not exist in complete isolation. It may receive things from outside itself, provide things to external counterparts, communicate with people or systems, request work, answer requests, exchange artifacts, participate in review, or maintain continuing external relationships.

Those relations occur at the surface of the bounded object. `L7/peering/` gives them an address without requiring the external counterpart to become part of the scientific interior.

[
	ext{inside object};|;	ext{surface relationship};|;	ext{external counterpart}
]

Peering represents the middle relation.

## Peering coordinates

Two independent distinctions organize the branch:

```text
L7/peering/
├── public/
│   ├── ingress/
│   └── egress/
└── private/
    ├── ingress/
    └── egress/
```

| Coordinate | What it describes |
|---|---|
| `public/` or `private/` | Exposure of the external relationship. |
| `ingress/` or `egress/` | Direction of the relationship relative to the bounded object. |
| `peer-N/` | One external counterpart distinguished within that channel. |
| `object-M/` | One object or interaction distinguished within that peer relation. |

These coordinates describe the relation. They do not establish truth, validity, authorship, ownership, permission, acceptance, importance, or scientific standing.

## Public and private are both auditable surface channels

`public/` and `private/` describe exposure, not whether a relationship exists.

### Public

A public peering relation is exposed through a public-facing channel. The relationship may therefore be inspected by the public or other openly admitted audience appropriate to that surface.

### Private

A private peering relation is exposed through a restricted channel. Its audience may be one person, a research group, an institution, an authenticated service, a review process, or another limited set of participants.

Private does not mean absent, unrepresented, or inherently unauditable. It remains an L7 surface relationship. Its audit audience is restricted rather than public.

Here **auditable** means that the represented relationship can be identified and inspected by the audience to which the channel is exposed. Peering does not prescribe an audit procedure, legal regime, or access-control implementation.

Therefore:

[
	ext{public}
eq	ext{auditable}
]

[
	ext{private}
eq	ext{unauditable}
]

Both are represented surface channels; their exposure differs.

## Ingress and egress

Direction is always stated relative to the bounded object.

### Ingress

[
	ext{external counterpart}ightarrow	ext{bounded object}
]

Ingress describes a relationship directed toward the bounded object. An external counterpart may be presenting, supplying, transmitting, requesting, returning, exposing, or otherwise directing an object or interaction toward it.

Ingress does not by itself mean that the incoming thing is accepted, adopted, trusted, incorporated, or scientifically valid.

### Egress

[
	ext{bounded object}ightarrow	ext{external counterpart}
]

Egress describes a relationship directed toward an external counterpart. The bounded object may be presenting, supplying, transmitting, requesting, returning, exposing, or otherwise directing an object or interaction outward.

Egress does not by itself mean publication, approval, endorsement, or scientific maturity.

## Peer and object positions

The general form is:

```text
L7/peering/{public|private}/{ingress|egress}/peer-N/object-M/
```

`peer-N/` identifies one external counterpart within the local peering channel. The number is an enumerator, not a rank, authority level, or universal identity. Matching numbers in different channels do not by themselves assert that the counterparts are identical; an implementation may record that relation separately when it matters.

`object-M/` identifies one object or interaction within the relation to that peer. It may be a document, dataset, definition, message, request, response, review, computational result, software artifact, service interaction, endpoint reference, physical-artifact record, or another bounded representation.

The number distinguishes instances. It does not by itself establish chronology, ownership, version, authority, status, or acceptance.

## Holds

- the public and private exposure channels;
- ingress and egress direction within each exposure channel;
- locally distinguished external counterparts at `peer-N/`;
- locally distinguished objects or interactions at `object-M/`;
- the fact and form of the represented external relationship.

## Does not hold

Peering does not, merely by representing a relationship, establish:

- authorship or ownership;
- source validity or scientific truth;
- acceptance, rejection, adoption, or review status;
- copyright, licence, permission, access, or restriction;
- provenance or prior-art relation;
- internal scientific meaning;
- a required workflow for another repository.

Where those questions matter, they are represented by the 7LM position that actually owns that question.

## Boundary with the rest of L7

| L7 position | Primary question |
|---|---|
| `provenance/` | Where did this object or claim come from, and what source relation is relevant? |
| `governance/` | What external conditions, rights, permissions, restrictions, or controls apply where relevant? |
| `peering/` | With what external counterpart is the bounded object relating, through what exposure and direction? |
| `allications/` | What outward act or externally situated occurrence is being represented? |

One real-world event may have several of these properties. Each position states only the property it owns.

## Boundary with the scientific interior

Peering does not decide where scientific content belongs internally.

An object can cross the surface through a peering relation without becoming a definition, state, equation, engineering specification, experiment, conclusion, or retained scientific memory merely because it crossed the boundary.

Likewise, scientific content can leave through peering without acquiring greater authority merely because it was externally exposed.

[
	ext{external relation}
eq	ext{scientific meaning}
]

If an externally encountered definition is adopted for use at L0, that is an L0 semantic fact. Its source may be represented through provenance. Peering is used only when the external relationship itself is part of what the bounded object represents. A definition found in a book, archive, database, or web search does not become a peer relation merely because it is external.

[
	ext{external source}
otRightarrow	ext{peer}
]

## Exemplars

These examples teach the coordinates. They do not add architectural requirements.

### High-school research project

A student sends a draft to a teacher through a restricted channel:

```text
L7/peering/private/egress/peer-1/object-1/
```

The teacher returns comments:

```text
L7/peering/private/ingress/peer-1/object-2/
```

The paths state the external relationship, exposure, direction, counterpart, and distinguished interaction. They do not decide whether the comments are correct or whether the student adopts them.

### College laboratory

A collaborating laboratory privately sends a calibration dataset:

```text
L7/peering/private/ingress/peer-1/object-1/
```

If the laboratories later discuss the result through a publicly visible repository issue, that public interaction may be represented separately through `public/`.

### Peer-review preparation

A manuscript sent to a journal through a restricted submission system may be represented as private egress. Reviewer comments returned through that system may be represented as private ingress. Publication, if it later occurs, is a distinct L7 outward act and is not created merely by the peering exchange.

### Public external interaction

A public repository receives a public issue from an external researcher:

```text
L7/peering/public/ingress/peer-1/object-1/
```

A public response to that counterpart is public egress.

### External but not peering

A researcher discovers a definition in a published source through a web search. Provenance may matter because the source contributed meaning to the research. Peering does not automatically apply because no external counterpart relationship is being represented.

## Standing

- This branch may remain empty when no peering relation is represented.
- Public/private and ingress/egress are semantic coordinates of peering; they are not mandatory vocabulary for every external implementation.
- The exemplars demonstrate uses of the coordinates. They do not restrict peering to those uses.
- Machine metadata may describe these coordinates in an implementation, but no machine metadata or automation is instantiated by this sheet.
